[1] During one of his usual nighttime walks through the empty industrial district, where the stood silent and streets shed more stones with each passing year, Julian came to the soot-blackened mill where Mr. Vane, an occasional associate, kept a private loft. Both men were archivists by trade—Julian spent his days with reams of old papers, whereas Vane was a collector of more obscure and fascinating objects. 

[2] Julian entered uninvited and found Mr. Vane was sitting on a wooden crate, quietly trimming his fingernails with a small knife. “You’re late,” he said, without looking up. 

Julian said nothing. His visits there were always unannounced, yet Mr. Vane always expected his arrival. 

[3] Dozens of wooden marionettes hung from the iron beams above. They were carved with great skill, yet still plain and unfinished. Their faces were pale, and their glass eyes reflected the weak light coming through the high windows.

“They’re nearly finished,” Vane said as he tucked away the knife. “Do they frighten you?’ He pointed toward one of the hanging figures. 

Julian shook his head. “They’re just things.” 

“No? Perhaps they should. They are things, yes. Empty things. Potential things. They’re waiting to become someone.”

[4] Julian walked closer. One puppet had broad wooden shoulders and large hands made from pale pine. As he studied the hints of features its maker had given it, he felt a knot of panic in his thin belly. The puppet’s stiff fingers looked exactly like his own after long nights spent turning the dry pages of old books. He wondered what would remain if everything that stained him were washed away—his memories, his habits, his little everyday worries. Would there be anything left except an empty shape, waiting to be given a purpose?

“They’re waiting for permission to suffer,” Julian whispered.

“Or simply waiting to be told who they are,” Vane replied. “Perhaps that is all anyone ever does.”

[5] Julian continued staring at the puppet.

In the dark, sickly air of Vane’s loft, the puppet’s face slid and shifted before him. In it, he now saw lonely workers, librarians, all the forgotten people of the city searching for meaning, none of them grasping why they felt so empty. Who held his own strings, Julian wondered.

Vane had become completely still, and Julian dared look no closer at him, fearing that he’d see a wooden man perched on that crate if he did. The room itself seemed to be speaking through silence.

[6] Julian felt his wrists growing stiff, his arm raising, his fingers reaching out, and as he watched his own body, he realized that no strings were pulling him. He was reaching out himself.  All his life he had searched for forgotten things, driven by curiosity even when it brought him no happiness. That same curiosity now made him reach toward the puppet, this potential being, which had to be made flesh not for its own sake, but for his. 

Slowly, he stretched out one finger until it almost touched the puppet’s wooden hand.

[7] When his finger finally brushed the puppet’s knuckle, the figure suddenly took a long, dry breath. Its glass eyes trembled, and in that glass, Julian saw the same fear he knew so well, the fear that often kept him up, pacing his cluttered room until the early hours of the morning.

He had completed the puppet himself. That was why he’d come, what Vane had needed him for.  As he stood beneath the hanging figures, each of them now twitching and gasping, trying to shake their wooden frames free of a terrible ache they’d never be rid of, another thought came to him. Perhaps he too had come about the same way. Perhaps someone, somewhere, had once reached into the darkness, a lonely architect hoping against hope that something would reach back.
