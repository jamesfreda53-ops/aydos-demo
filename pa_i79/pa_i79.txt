[1] The air tasted of sulfur, stale diesel, and the bitter dust of crumbling brickwork—the permanent perfume of a town that had spent the last forty years on its deathbed. Tess stood over the crossbar of her ten-speed, her sneakers planted firmly on the oil-stained asphalt of the Interstate 79 Motor Lodge. The plastic ice machine out front had cracked its casing in the nineties and never been fixed, humming a dull, vibrating bass against the hollow core-doors of the rooms.

Inside #8, there were no moving boxes because there was nothing to pack. Tess and her mother lived out of two scuffed vinyl suitcases and a pair of heavy-duty Hefty bags, their entire earthly inventory lightweight enough to be tossed into the trunk of a dying Ford without scraping the shocks. Her mother had finally managed a transfer to a dry-cleaner warehouse three hours north—somewhere with actual municipal water that didn't run brown after a heavy rain, and a landlord willing to take a chance on a deposit. It was a lifeline, clean and undeniable.

[2] Yet, as Tess pushed off and pedaled down the shoulder of the highway, the bleakness of the landscape felt less like a background and more like a second skin.

She rode past the carcass of the old glass plant, its shattered windows staring blankly across overgrown lots choked with Queen Anne's lace and rusted rebar. There was little nostalgia here, no weathered barns or Rockwell diners; just miles of skeletal conveyor belts, boarded-up storefronts with peeling "Everything Must Go" signs faded to ghost-text, and the heavy, crushing weight of a town that had been abandoned by the corporate map decades ago.

[3] She slowed as she approached the rusted chain-link fence of the rail yard, where Charlie was already waiting. His daddy ran a pawnshop, and he had a knack for fixing up broken things. She'd always thought he'd be the one to make it out. Charlie leaned against the fence, wearing his jacket despite the weather, holding a can of iced tea pilfered from the cooler at the Dollar General.

[4] "I brought one for you," Charlie said, handing her a green, perspiring can.
"Thanks," Tess said, letting the bike collapse into the tall grass. "My mom wants me back early tonight."

[5] Charlie nodded slowly, looking out over the silent tracks that hadn't seen an active freight line in two summers. They drank their sweet tea together, saying very little, and it struck Tess that she'd never really learned how to say goodbye. 

[6]  She took in the fat flies baking in the sun on the side of the boxcars, the smoke stacks of the abandoned coke plant cutting jagged teeth into the low clouds, the peach fuzz sprouting on Charlie's chin, the grease stains on his jacket. 

[7] In her mind, she snapped photographs. Neither of them could say the words, so these stolen images would have to do.
